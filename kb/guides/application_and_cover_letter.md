# The Application Package and the Cover Letter

Reference material for the German and wider European engineering job market.
Complements `ats_guide.md`, which covers parsing and keyword strategy; this
covers what goes around the CV and how the letter is constructed.

## What a complete application contains

In Germany the expected package is more than a CV:

- **Anschreiben** — the cover letter. One page. Still expected by most employers,
  and read, unlike in some markets.
- **Lebenslauf** — the CV. Reverse-chronological, typically two pages.
- **Zeugnisse** — certificates and references: degree certificates, and employment
  references (Arbeitszeugnisse) from previous employers.

International and startup employers increasingly ask only for a CV. Where the
posting is in German and lists Bewerbungsunterlagen, send the full set.

A note on photographs: historically standard, and now legally fraught under the
General Equal Treatment Act, so many employers no longer expect one. Omitting it
is safe; including it is still common in traditional industries. Date of birth
and marital status are no longer expected and can be left out.

## The cover letter's actual job

Not to summarise the CV. The reader has the CV.

It answers three questions, in this order:

1. **Why this role** — evidence you understood the posting rather than sending the
   same letter everywhere
2. **Why you can do it** — two or three specific pieces of evidence, drawn from
   the CV but expanded with detail the CV had no room for
3. **Why this employer** — something specific and true about them

Question three is where most letters fail. "I am impressed by your innovative
culture" is a sentence about no company in particular. A reference to a product,
a market, a published position or a technology they have committed to is worth
more than three paragraphs of enthusiasm.

## Structure that works

- **Subject line** — role title and the reference number from the posting
- **Opening** — one sentence: the role, and the single strongest reason you are
  writing. Not "I am writing to apply for", which spends the most-read line on
  nothing.
- **Body, two or three short paragraphs** — one piece of evidence each, matched to
  a requirement from the posting. Say what you did, what it was measured in, and
  what changed.
- **Employer paragraph** — the specific thing, above
- **Close** — availability, notice period, and a plain statement that you would
  welcome an interview

One page. Twelve-point body text. Named recipient where the posting gives one.

## Matching the letter to the posting

Work from the posting rather than from your CV:

1. List the requirements it states, separating must-haves from nice-to-haves
2. For each must-have, find your strongest concrete evidence
3. Where you have none, decide whether it is adjacent — something you have done
   that transfers — or genuinely absent
4. Write to the top two or three. A letter covering eight requirements covers none
   of them convincingly.

## Addressing a gap you actually have

Do not pretend, and do not over-explain. One clause, factual, immediately followed
by what is adjacent:

> The posting asks for Rust; my systems work has been in C++ and Go, and I have
> been working through the Rust book on the embedded track since spring.

That is more persuasive than silence, because the reader has already noticed, and
far more persuasive than claiming familiarity that the interview will disprove in
ninety seconds.

The same applies to employment gaps: name the period and the reason in one line
in the CV, and move on. An unexplained gap invites a worse assumption than most
real explanations.

## The Arbeitszeugnis

German employment references are written in a coded language, and it matters
because you will be asked for them and a future employer will read them properly.

The convention is that the reference must be benevolent in tone and truthful in
content, so criticism is expressed through **grading formulas**. Roughly:

- *stets zu unserer vollsten Zufriedenheit* — excellent
- *stets zu unserer vollen Zufriedenheit* — good
- *zu unserer vollen Zufriedenheit* — satisfactory
- *zu unserer Zufriedenheit* — adequate, and read as poor

Note the pattern: the strength of the phrase carries the grade. The absence of an
expected element — no closing thanks, no regret at the departure, no good wishes —
is itself a signal.

Read yours when you receive it. You are entitled to a qualified reference, and if
the wording grades you below what was actually said to you, it can be disputed.

## Interview rounds in German engineering firms

Commonly: a recruiter screen, then a technical round with the hiring team, then a
round with a manager and often HR together. Larger manufacturers add a structured
competency interview, and some use an assessment centre for graduate entry.

Two things that differ from an American-style process: punctuality is treated as
information about you, and directness is not rudeness — a blunt technical
challenge is usually a genuine question rather than a pressure test.

## Practical observations

- **A tailored letter is visibly tailored in the first two sentences or not at
  all.** The reader decides there.
- **Do not restate the CV in prose.** It reads as padding and wastes the one page.
- **Send PDF, named sensibly** — `Lastname_Firstname_Anschreiben.pdf`. A file
  called `letter_final_v3.pdf` is a small, avoidable signal.
- **Match the language of the posting.** A German posting answered in English
  suggests you did not read it, unless the posting says English is fine.
- **Keep a record of what you sent where.** Three weeks later, a recruiter calls
  about a role you applied to among nine others, and knowing which version of the
  CV they hold is the difference between a good call and a vague one.
