# Salary Research and Negotiation

Reference material for the German and wider European engineering job market.
Figures move; the method does not. Nothing here is legal or financial advice.

## Establish a range before anyone asks

Negotiation is mostly research. A candidate who has done it answers the salary
question with a range and a reason; one who has not either anchors low or names a
number they cannot justify.

Sources, roughly in order of usefulness:

- **Collective agreements.** In German engineering and automotive, IG Metall
  agreements set pay groups for many employers. Where a company is bound by one,
  the band is public and the negotiation is about which group you land in, not
  about the number.
- **Published salary reports** from recruiters and industry bodies, read for the
  shape rather than the precision.
- **Job postings that state a range.** Increasingly common, and directly
  comparable.
- **People doing the job.** Former colleagues, meetups, alumni. The least
  systematic and the most accurate.
- **Aggregator sites.** Wide bands, self-reported, often stale — useful only as a
  sanity check.

Adjust for the things that genuinely move the number: region (Munich and
Stuttgart run well above the national median, the east below), company size,
industry, and whether the role is at a manufacturer, a tier-one supplier or a
consultancy.

## Understand the whole package before comparing

A higher gross is not automatically a better offer. What varies:

- **Bonus** — target versus guaranteed, company performance versus individual
- **13th month or holiday pay**, common under collective agreements
- **Working time** — a 35-hour week against a 40-hour one is a 14% difference in
  hourly terms that never appears in the headline figure
- **Holiday** — 30 days is standard; fewer is worth pricing
- **Pension contribution** (betriebliche Altersvorsorge)
- **Company car or mobility budget**, which carries a taxable benefit
- **Relocation, training budget, conference days**
- **Overtime treatment** — paid, compensated in time, or absorbed into salary

Comparing two offers means comparing totals per hour, not two gross figures.

## Who names a number first

The common advice is "never go first", which does not survive contact with German
recruiting: many applications ask for a salary expectation (Gehaltsvorstellung) as
a filter, and declining to answer can end the process.

What works better than refusing:

- Give a **range**, with the bottom being a number you would genuinely accept
- Base the top on research you can cite if asked
- Attach a condition: "this assumes the on-call arrangement described in the
  posting"
- If you have a strong reason to defer, defer *once* and briefly, then answer

A range of roughly 10–15% between floor and ceiling reads as informed. A range of
40% reads as a guess.

## When the offer arrives

- **Do not accept on the call.** "Thank you — may I have it in writing and come
  back tomorrow?" is normal and costs nothing.
- **Get it in writing** before negotiating. Verbal figures have a way of shifting.
- **Negotiate once, on everything at once.** A sequence of separate asks is
  exhausting for the employer and reads as bad faith. One message covering salary,
  start date and whatever else matters is efficient and respected.
- **Ask for a specific number**, not "a bit more". Specific numbers get specific
  answers.
- **Give a reason that is about the market or the role**, not about your costs.
  "The band for this scope in Stuttgart is X" is an argument; "I have a mortgage"
  is not.

## When salary genuinely cannot move

It often cannot, especially under a collective agreement or a rigid internal
band. That is the moment to ask what *can*:

- A higher pay group, if the scope justifies one
- An earlier first review — six months rather than twelve — **in writing**
- Training budget, a certification, conference attendance
- Additional holiday or a formalised remote arrangement
- A signing bonus, which often comes from a different budget than salary

"The salary is fixed" is rarely the same sentence as "nothing is available".

## Internal raises

Different game. The decision is usually made weeks before the conversation, in a
budget round, so timing beats argument.

- Ask **before** the budget cycle closes, not at the review
- Bring evidence of scope that has grown, not effort that was expended
- Compare against the market band for the job you are now doing
- Agree a concrete target if the answer is "not yet" — a date and a condition,
  written down

## Common mistakes

- **Anchoring on the current salary.** A new employer is buying the role, not
  matching your history. Under-market pay follows you if you let it.
- **Treating a first offer as final.** Many contain deliberate room.
- **Negotiating hard after enthusiastic acceptance.** The leverage was before
  "yes".
- **Ignoring the hours.** See above; it is the most commonly missed 10%.
- **Burning goodwill for a small gain.** The people negotiating will be your
  colleagues on Monday.

## Practical observations

- **Silence is a tool and it is uncomfortable.** After naming a number, stop
  talking. The pause usually gets filled by the other side.
- **Everything agreed verbally should appear in the contract.** A remote
  arrangement that lives only in a hiring manager's memory does not survive that
  manager leaving.
- **Check the notice period and probation terms** with the same attention as the
  salary; in Germany they materially affect how quickly you could move again.
- **A withdrawn offer over a polite, well-reasoned counter is rare** — and where
  it happens, it has told you something useful about the employer.
