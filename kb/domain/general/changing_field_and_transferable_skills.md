# Changing Field, and What Actually Transfers

Reference material for any career track. Part of the **General** profile's
folder, which covers the ground the three specialised tracks do not.

## The problem, stated honestly

A career change asks a reader to take a risk. They can see what you have done;
they are being asked to believe you will be good at something adjacent. Most
applications lose here not because the change is implausible but because the
candidate argues the wrong case — they explain their *motivation* when the
reader wants evidence of *capability*.

Motivation matters, but it is the second question. The first is: can this person
do the job on Monday.

## What transfers, and what does not

Transferable is a much narrower word than it is usually used as. Three
categories, and only one of them is worth leading with.

**Genuinely transferable — lead with these.** Skills exercised in your old field
that the new one uses in the same form:

- Reading a specification and finding what it does not say
- Root-cause analysis on a failure you did not cause
- Owning a measurement and defending it to someone who dislikes the result
- Working with a supplier, a customer or a regulator
- Breaking a large problem into steps someone else can pick up

**Domain-adjacent — worth naming, not worth leading with.** Knowledge that
shortens the learning curve without replacing it: understanding how a production
line fails helps in manufacturing software and does not make you a software
engineer.

**Claimed but not transferable — leave out.** Tool familiarity from a tutorial,
a course with no applied project, or seniority itself. Seniority does not
transfer across fields; the judgement that produced it partly does, and only
through examples.

## The structure that works

1. **Open with the capability, in the new field's language.** Not "I am changing
   from mechanical engineering to data" — that is about you. "Nine years finding
   why measurements drift, now doing it in Python against production logs" is
   about the work.
2. **Two or three pieces of evidence**, each drawn from real work, each mapped to
   something the posting asks for.
3. **One honest sentence about the gap**, and what you are doing about it. Named
   and bounded beats unmentioned.
4. **Then the motivation**, briefly. One or two sentences. It is the part that
   feels most important to write and matters least to the reader.

## Translating vocabulary

Every field has its own words for the same activity, and a CV left in the old
field's vocabulary reads as someone who has not arrived yet. A few common
translations:

- *8D report* → structured incident investigation
- *measurement systems analysis* → validating that your instrument is not the
  source of the variance
- *tolerance band* → acceptance threshold
- *end-of-line test* → automated acceptance test before release
- *supplier audit* → third-party assurance review

Translate, do not disguise. The original term often belongs in brackets, because
a reader who knows it reads it as credibility rather than jargon.

## Evidence you can build before you have the job

The honest weakness in a change is that you cannot show the new work until
somebody lets you do it. What actually closes that:

- **A finished small thing beats a large unfinished one.** One project, deployed
  or published, with a README explaining the decision you made and the one you
  rejected.
- **Use your old domain as the subject.** A data project about manufacturing
  defects is far more persuasive from a manufacturing engineer than another
  tutorial dataset, because it demonstrates both sides at once.
- **Contribute something small and real** — a documentation fix, a bug report
  with a reproduction. It shows you can work inside someone else's standards.
- **Certifications establish a floor, not a ceiling.** They answer "has this
  person covered the basics" and never "is this person good".

## Internal moves

Usually the shortest route, and the most underused. You are already trusted, your
record is visible, and the risk to the employer is smaller.

- Do part of the new work before asking for the title — the overlap where the two
  roles meet is nearly always available
- Find the person who owns the work rather than going through process first
- Ask for a defined trial with an end date; it is much easier to agree to than a
  permanent change

## What interviewers actually probe

- **Whether you understand what you are walking into**, including the dull parts.
  Enthusiasm for the interesting 10% reads as naivety.
- **Whether you can learn in public.** A specific example of being wrong and
  correcting it does more here than in most interviews.
- **Whether you will leave when it is harder than expected.** The answer is
  evidence of having stuck with something difficult before, not a promise.

## Practical observations

- **Do not apologise.** A CV that opens by explaining a deficit frames everything
  after it as compensation.
- **Apply to the job below the one you want**, once, to see the questions. The
  feedback is worth more than another month of preparation.
- **Expect a pay step down, and decide your floor before the conversation.**
  Going in without a number is how a temporary step down becomes permanent.
- **The first role after a change is the hard one.** The second is judged on the
  first, not on the old field.
