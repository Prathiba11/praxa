# Writing Evidence Rather Than Duties

Reference material for any career track. Domain-neutral on purpose: this is the
folder behind the **General** profile, which exists for CVs the three
specialised tracks do not cover.

## The difference that decides most CVs

Two lines describing the same job:

> Responsible for test automation of drive unit assemblies.

> Rebuilt the end-of-line test rig for a drive unit family, cutting cycle time
> from 340 to 195 seconds and removing two manual handling steps.

The first is a **duty** — it says what you were assigned. The second is
**evidence** — it says what changed because you were there. A reader can argue
with the second one, which is precisely why it is worth more: a claim that could
be checked reads as true, and a claim that could not be checked reads as filler.

Most CVs are lists of duties, because duties are what appear in a job
description and a job description is the easiest thing to copy.

## Turning a duty into evidence

For each line, ask three questions in order:

1. **What was the situation before?** A number if one exists, a description if
   not. "Deployments took nineteen minutes." "Nobody owned incoming inspection."
2. **What did *you* do?** Not the team. If the honest answer is "I was one of
   six", say so — a credible small claim beats an unbelievable large one.
3. **What was different afterwards, and how do you know?** The measurement, or
   the observable consequence.

If question three has no answer, the line may still belong on the CV, but as a
scope statement rather than an achievement. "Owned the test specification for a
240-requirement programme across four variants" is a legitimate line: it
establishes scale without claiming an outcome it cannot support.

## Numbers, and how not to abuse them

Numbers are persuasive, which is exactly why they get invented. Some rules that
survive an interview:

- **Use the number you actually know.** If you know the before and after of a
  cycle time, use it. If you only know "about half", write "roughly half" — a
  hedged real number is stronger than a precise fictional one, because you can
  defend it.
- **Prefer the number that is yours.** Company revenue is not your achievement.
  The defect rate on your line is.
- **Give the denominator.** "Reduced failures by 40%" means nothing without
  knowing whether that was 10 failures or 10,000.
- **Do not turn everything into a percentage.** A CV where every line ends in a
  percentage reads as a template, and reviewers start discounting all of them.

If a number would need to be looked up and cannot be, describe the change
qualitatively and precisely: "removed the manual transcription step, which was
the source of the audit findings" is fine.

## Scope, and why it is separate from outcome

Two different things a reader wants to know: how big was the thing you worked
on, and what happened to it. They deserve separate treatment.

Scope is expressed in the units of your field — services, requirements,
suppliers, variants, users, lines, machines, students. One scope line near the
top of a role, then outcome lines under it, reads more clearly than mixing them.

## Ordering within a role

Strongest first, and "strongest" means most relevant to the posting you are
answering, not most impressive in the abstract. A reader gives the first two
bullets of a role real attention and skims the rest, so a brilliant fourth bullet
is nearly invisible.

This is the main thing tailoring does: the same role, reordered.

## Gaps, changes and short tenures

Each one has a shape that works:

- **A gap** — name the period and the reason in one clause, then move on.
  Unexplained gaps get worse assumptions than most real explanations.
- **A career change** — lead with the transferable evidence, not with the
  narrative of why you changed. What you can do now matters more than how you
  came to be able to do it.
- **A short tenure** — leave it in. A missing year is more noticeable than a
  short one, and being asked about it is a normal conversation.
- **Contract or agency work** — group it under one heading with the agency named
  once, rather than listing six three-month entries that look like instability.

## What to leave out

- Duties that every holder of the title performs
- Skills sections listing things you used once
- Soft-skill adjectives with no evidence attached — "excellent communicator"
  costs a line and proves nothing, while "presented monthly to the programme
  lead" proves it in passing
- Anything you could not discuss for five minutes under questioning

## Practical observations

- **Write the evidence line before deciding whether it fits.** It is much easier
  to cut a good line than to improve a vague one.
- **The interview is the audit.** Every number on the page is a question you have
  invited. That is a good trade if you can answer it and a bad one if you cannot.
- **Keep a long version.** One file with every achievement you have, unpruned,
  and tailor down from it. Tailoring from an already-short CV loses material
  permanently.
- **Watch the tense.** Current role in present tense, everything else in past.
  Mixed tense is the most common sign of a document edited over several years.
